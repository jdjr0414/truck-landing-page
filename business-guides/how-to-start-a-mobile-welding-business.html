How to Start a Mobile Welding Business

Starting a mobile welding business requires a service truck equipped with a welder, welding equipment, and tools. This guide walks through forming your business, choosing a welding niche (structural, pipe, fabrication, repair), purchasing or financing a service truck with welder, securing insurance, and building a customer base. Overview

A mobile welding business provides on-site welding, fabrication, and repair services. Revenue comes from hourly rates, project fees, and emergency call-outs. Success depends on welding skills, equipment reliability, and customer relationships. Common niches include structural welding, pipe welding, agricultural repair, manufacturing support, and fleet maintenance. Many operators start solo and scale with additional trucks or employees.

Customers and Revenue

Primary customers: construction contractors, farmers, manufacturers, oilfield operators, fleet managers, and municipalities. Revenue from hourly rates ($75–$150+ per hour), project-based pricing, and emergency service premiums. Construction and agriculture provide seasonal peaks; manufacturing and fleet work offer more consistent demand. Building relationships with project managers and maintenance supervisors drives referrals and repeat business.

Equipment

Core equipment is a service truck with an integrated welder—either engine-driven or generator-powered. Service trucks with welding packages: $40,000–$120,000 new; $25,000–$70,000 used. Welding equipment includes the welder (stick, MIG, TIG, or multi-process), torch, grinders, clamps, and hand tools. See service truck financing and utility truck financing.

Typical Equipment Needed

  • Service truck with welder (engine-driven or generator)
  • Welding machine (stick, MIG, TIG, or multi-process)
  • Torch, grinders, clamps
  • Hand and power tools
  • Safety equipment (helmet, gloves, respirator)

Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

Mobile welding operators must meet state and local requirements. See commercial truck license requirements.

CDL: Depends on truck weight. Service trucks under 26,001 lbs GVWR may not require CDL. Over 26,001 lbs typically requires Class B CDL. Heavier rigs with crane or large welder may exceed limits—verify your configuration.

DOT: USDOT number if interstate. Many mobile welding operations are intrastate.

State and local: Business registration. Welding certifications (AWS, etc.) are often required by clients but not always by law. Some industrial and pipeline work requires certified welders.

OSHA and specialized: Confined space, hot work permits, and safety training may apply on job sites. Client requirements often exceed minimum legal standards.

Disclaimer: Licensing requirements vary by state and client. Verify with your state and local authorities before operating.

Typical License Requirements

  • Class B CDL (if GVWR exceeds 26,000 lbs)
  • Welding certifications (client-dependent)
  • State business registration

Insurance

Commercial auto liability, general liability, and possibly professional liability. Industrial and fleet clients often require $1M+ limits and additional insured status. Workers comp if you have employees.

Typical Insurance Needs

  • Commercial auto liability
  • General liability
  • Workers comp (if employees)

Financing

Service truck financing is available from specialty commercial lenders. Down payment varies by credit—strong credit may qualify for low or no down payment; new businesses often need 20–30%. Proof of contracts or letters of intent strengthens applications. Loan terms typically 48–72 months for trucks with welding packages.

Common Mistakes

Starting without a defined niche—structural, pipe, and agricultural welding have different equipment and client needs. Undercapitalization; consumables and slow client payment strain cash flow. Underpricing—hourly rates must cover truck, equipment, and overhead. Failing to verify CDL requirements for your specific truck weight. Skipping insurance verification before bidding on industrial contracts.
