How to Start a Mobile RV Service Business

Starting a mobile RV service business requires a service truck, RV repair tools, and strong relationships with RV parks and owners. This guide walks through forming your business, purchasing or financing a service truck, equipping for RV repair and maintenance, and building a customer base. Revenue comes from on-site repairs, maintenance, and diagnostics. Most operators do not need a CDL for smaller service trucks.

Quick Answer

To start a mobile RV service business: form an LLC, get RV repair training or certification, purchase or finance a service truck ($40,000–$100,000), equip with RV repair tools, get insurance, and build relationships with RV parks and owners. Startup costs: $55,000–$120,000. Usually no CDL for smaller trucks. Down payment varies by credit—not always required. See service truck financing.

Overview

A mobile RV service business provides on-site repair and maintenance for recreational vehicles. Technicians drive to RV parks, campgrounds, storage lots, or customer locations to fix electrical systems, plumbing, HVAC, appliances, slide-outs, and structural issues. Revenue comes from labor rates and parts markup. Success depends on reliable equipment, technical skills, customer service, and partnerships with RV parks and dealerships.

Customers and Revenue

Primary customers: RV park and campground guests, full-time RVers, seasonal owners, and RV dealerships. RV parks often maintain preferred vendor lists—getting on those lists drives steady work. Revenue from hourly labor ($100–$175+), diagnostic fees, and parts. Emergency and after-hours service commands premium rates. Building relationships with park managers and campground owners is critical for referrals.

Equipment

Core equipment is a service truck with compartments for tools, parts, and diagnostic equipment. See service truck financing. Vehicle cost: $40,000–$100,000 new or used. RV repair requires electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and appliance tools. Most mobile RV service trucks are under 26,000 lbs GVWR—no CDL required for smaller units.

Typical Equipment Needed

  • Service truck with service body
  • Electrical and multimeter tools
  • Plumbing and propane tools
  • HVAC and appliance repair tools
  • Parts inventory (common RV components)
  • Scheduling and invoicing software

Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

Mobile RV service has fewer formal licensing requirements than some trades. See commercial truck license requirements for vehicle rules.

CDL: Usually not required for smaller trucks. Service trucks under 26,000 lbs GVWR typically use a standard driver's license.

Trade licensing: No universal RV technician license. Some states require contractor or handyman licensing for certain work. RVIA certification (RV Technical Institute) is voluntary but strengthens credibility.

Business registration: LLC or corporation, business license, and insurance.

Disclaimer: Licensing requirements vary by state. Verify with your state licensing board before operating.

Typical License Requirements

  • Standard driver's license (no CDL for most trucks)
  • State business registration
  • RVIA or manufacturer certification (optional but recommended)

Insurance

Commercial auto liability, general liability, and professional liability (errors and omissions) for repair work. Parts and inventory coverage if carrying significant stock. Workers comp if you have employees.

Typical Insurance Needs

  • Commercial auto liability
  • General liability
  • Professional liability (E&O)
  • Workers comp (if employees)

Financing

Service truck financing is available from commercial lenders. Down payment varies by credit—strong credit may qualify for low or no down payment; new businesses often need 15–25%. See commercial vehicle financing for options. Loan terms typically 48–72 months for service trucks.

Common Mistakes

Undercapitalization; parts inventory and tool investment add up quickly. Failing to build RV park relationships—parks are a primary source of customers. Poor scheduling and long drive times. Underestimating seasonal demand (peak in spring and fall). Skipping professional liability insurance. Not carrying common parts—customers expect faster turnaround.
