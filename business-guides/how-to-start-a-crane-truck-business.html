How to Start a Crane Truck Business

Starting a crane truck business requires a truck-mounted crane, operator certification, and contractor or utility contracts. This guide walks through forming your business, obtaining crane certification, purchasing or financing a crane truck, securing insurance, and finding lifting and placement work. Revenue comes from hourly rates and project-based contracts.

Quick Answer

To start a crane truck business: form an LLC, obtain crane operator certification (NCCCO), purchase or finance a crane truck ($150,000–$400,000+ new), get insurance, and secure contracts with construction, utility, or sign companies. Startup costs: $100,000–$350,000+. Down payment varies by credit—not always required. See crane truck financing and how much does a crane truck cost.

Overview

A crane truck business provides mobile lifting and placement services using a truck-mounted crane. Services include material placement, equipment installation, sign erection, and utility work. Success depends on certified operators, reliable equipment, and contractor relationships. Crane capacity (tons) and reach determine job scope and rates.

Customers and Revenue

Primary customers: construction companies, utilities, sign installers, equipment rental companies, and general contractors. Revenue from hourly rates ($125–$250+ per hour) or per-lift fees. Building relationships with project managers and subcontracting through established contractors drives steady work. Seasonal demand in construction.

Equipment

Core equipment is a truck-mounted crane (knuckle boom or articulating crane). Capacity ranges from 5 to 30+ tons. See how much does a crane truck cost. Crane truck financing is available. New: $150,000–$400,000+; used: $80,000–$250,000. See boom truck financing for related equipment.

Typical Equipment Needed

  • Crane truck (knuckle boom or articulating)
  • Rigging equipment (slings, chains)
  • Outrigger pads
  • Trailer (for equipment transport)

Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

Crane operators must meet CDL and crane certification requirements. See commercial truck license requirements and heavy equipment operator certification.

CDL: Crane trucks over 26,000 lbs GVWR typically require Class A or Class B CDL.

DOT: USDOT number if interstate. Many operations are intrastate.

State and local: Business registration. Some states require crane operator certification. Oversize/overweight permits for transport.

OSHA and specialized: NCCCO or state-required crane operator certification. OSHA requires certified operators for most cranes.

Disclaimer: Licensing and certification requirements vary by state. Verify with your state and OSHA before operating.

Typical License Requirements

  • Class A or Class B CDL
  • NCCCO or state crane operator certification
  • State business registration

Insurance

Commercial auto liability, general liability, and rigging/equipment liability. Contractors often require $2M+ limits. Workers comp required if you have employees.

Typical Insurance Needs

  • Commercial auto liability
  • General liability ($2M+ for contractors)
  • Rigging/equipment liability
  • Workers comp (if employees)

Financing

Crane truck financing is available from specialty commercial lenders. Down payment varies by credit—strong credit may qualify for low or no down payment; new businesses often need 20–30%. Proof of contracts strengthens applications. Loan terms typically 48–72 months.

Common Mistakes

Operating without crane certification—OSHA requires it. Undercapitalization; contractor payment cycles run 30–90 days. Buying oversized crane for initial market. Skipping rigging training. Underinsuring; contractors require high liability limits.
