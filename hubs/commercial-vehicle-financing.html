Commercial Vehicle Financing

How to Finance Commercial Trucks and Specialty Vehicles

Commercial vehicle financing covers semi trucks, dump trucks, bucket trucks, vac trucks, and dozens of vocational and specialty vehicles used by businesses. This guide explains how financing works, what lenders consider, and how to structure deals for new and used equipment.

What Is Commercial Vehicle Financing?

Commercial vehicle financing is lending secured by trucks, trailers, and specialty equipment used for business purposes. Unlike consumer auto loans, commercial financing is underwritten on business credit, revenue, and cash flow. Lenders include banks, credit unions, captive finance companies, and specialty commercial lenders. Structures range from traditional loans to leases, and terms typically run 24 to 84 months depending on equipment type and age.

Types of Commercial Vehicles Financed

Commercial vehicles fall into several categories, each with different financing considerations.

Over-the-Road Trucks

Semi trucks, tractors, and heavy-duty haulers used for freight, logistics, and long-haul operations. Financing for semi truck financing and heavy-haul truck financing typically allows higher amounts and longer terms due to strong collateral value. Day cab semi financing and sleeper truck financing follow similar structures with variations for regional vs. long-haul use.

Construction and Hauling Trucks

Dump truck financing, cement truck financing, and flatbed truck financing serve construction, aggregate, and materials hauling. Lenders assess revenue from contracts, haul rates, and seasonal patterns. Logging truck financing and tanker truck financing are specialized niches with equipment-specific valuation.

Vocational and Utility Trucks

Bucket truck financing, vac truck financing, and service truck financing support utilities, tree care, telecom, and field service. Utility truck financing often includes body and equipment add-ons. These units have strong residual value when well-maintained.

Specialty and Municipal Vehicles

Fire truck financing, garbage truck financing, crane truck financing, and snow plow truck financing serve municipal, waste, and specialized industrial applications. Financing structures account for higher unit costs and longer useful life.

Delivery and Logistics

Box truck financing, refrigerated truck financing, and tow truck financing support delivery, cold chain, and roadside service. Terms and advance rates vary by equipment age and revenue model.

How Commercial Vehicle Financing Works

Lenders evaluate three primary factors: the borrower, the equipment, and the structure.

Borrower Profile

  • Time in business: Most lenders prefer 12–24 months minimum; stronger programs require 2+ years.
  • Revenue: Annual revenue and cash flow support the payment. Lenders often require revenue documentation.
  • Credit: Personal and business credit affect rate and approval. Some programs accommodate lower scores with higher down payments.
  • Industry experience: Prior experience in trucking or the relevant industry can strengthen applications.

Equipment Factors

  • Age and mileage: Newer, lower-mileage units qualify for better terms. Used equipment over 5–7 years may face shorter terms or higher rates.
  • Condition: Inspections and appraisals confirm value. Well-maintained equipment supports stronger advances.
  • Specialty vs. standard: Common truck types (semi, dump, box) have clearer resale markets than highly specialized units.

Structure Options

  • Term loans: Fixed payments over 24–84 months. Equipment secures the loan.
  • Equipment leases: Operating leases for flexibility; capital leases for ownership. Lease terms often match useful life.
  • Balloon structures: Lower monthly payments with a larger final payment. Useful for cash flow management.

Down Payment and Advance Rates

Down payments typically range from 10% to 30% depending on credit, equipment age, and program. New equipment from dealers often allows 10–15% down for qualified borrowers. Used equipment, especially older units, may require 20–30%. Advance rates—the percentage of equipment value financed—usually cap at 80–90% for new and 70–80% for used. Strong applications can secure better terms.

New vs. Used Commercial Vehicle Financing

New trucks and vocational vehicles generally qualify for longer terms (60–84 months), lower rates, and higher advance rates. Manufacturer programs and captive lenders often offer promotional rates. Used equipment financing is more restrictive: shorter terms (24–60 months), higher rates, and lower advances. Lenders focus on remaining useful life and resale value.

Fleet and Multi-Unit Financing

Fleet acquisitions—multiple trucks or replacement cycles—often use dedicated fleet programs. Lenders may offer volume pricing, streamlined documentation, and master agreements. See the truck financing guide for fleet-specific structures and heavy-haul truck financing for large fleet applications.

Who Offers Commercial Vehicle Financing?

  • Banks and credit unions: Traditional lenders with commercial lending divisions. Often competitive for established businesses.
  • Captive finance companies: OEM-affiliated lenders (e.g., PACCAR Financial, Navistar Financial) with programs tied to dealer purchases.
  • Specialty commercial lenders: Non-bank lenders focused on equipment financing. Often more flexible on credit and structure.
  • Brokers and marketplaces: Connect borrowers with multiple lenders. Useful for comparing options and finding niche programs.

Rates and Terms by Equipment Type

Rates vary by equipment category. Semi trucks and standard vocational trucks typically qualify for the most competitive terms due to strong collateral markets. Specialty vehicles—fire trucks, airport equipment, rail maintenance trucks—may carry higher rates due to narrower resale markets. Terms generally run 36–84 months for new equipment and 24–60 months for used. Rates as of 2025 typically range from 7% to 15% APR for qualified commercial borrowers, with promotional programs sometimes offering lower rates on new dealer purchases.

Tax and Accounting Considerations

Section 179 and bonus depreciation can reduce taxable income for equipment purchases. Consult a tax advisor for current limits and eligibility. Leases may offer different treatment than loans. Document the business use of the vehicle and maintain records for IRS compliance. Some states impose use tax on equipment; factor this into total cost.

Refinancing and Restructuring

Existing commercial truck loans can often be refinanced when rates drop or business credit improves. Refinancing may lower payments, extend terms, or free up cash. Some lenders allow add-ons—financing additional equipment or improvements under the same note. Restructuring is an option when cash flow is tight; discuss forbearance or modification with your lender before missing payments.

Red Flags for Lenders

Lenders scrutinize recent bankruptcies, tax liens, and excessive debt. Multiple recent inquiries can raise concerns. Inconsistent revenue or declining cash flow may limit approval. Equipment with unclear title, salvage history, or significant modifications can complicate financing. Disclose issues upfront; surprises during underwriting often delay or derail deals.

Owner-Operator and Startup Financing

Owner-operators and new trucking businesses face stricter underwriting. Lenders often require proof of contracts, load history, or letters of intent. Some programs are designed for first-time truck buyers with strong personal credit and a solid business plan. Down payments of 20–30% are common for new entrants. See our truck financing guide for more on requirements and semi truck financing for owner-operator specifics.

Industry-Specific Programs

Certain industries have specialized financing. Waste and recycling fleets, municipal vehicles, and oilfield equipment may qualify for programs tailored to those sectors. Environmental services, utility contractors, and telecommunications often use bucket trucks, vac trucks, and specialty bodies—lenders familiar with these assets can offer better terms. Construction firms commonly use dump trucks and cement trucks; waste haulers use garbage trucks.

Getting Started

Gather business documentation (tax returns, financials, bank statements), equipment details (make, model, year, VIN, price), and a clear use case. Compare programs from multiple lenders. Frequently Asked Questions

What credit score do I need for commercial truck financing?

Many programs require a personal FICO of 650 or higher for competitive terms. Some specialty lenders work with scores in the 580–650 range, often with higher down payments or shorter terms. Business credit and revenue can offset weaker personal scores.

Can I finance a used commercial truck?

Yes. Used commercial truck financing is widely available. Terms are typically shorter (24–60 months) than for new equipment, and advance rates may be lower. Lenders consider age, mileage, condition, and remaining useful life.

How much down payment is required?

Down payments typically range from 10% to 30%. New equipment from dealers often allows 10–15% for qualified borrowers. Used or older equipment may require 20–30%. Strong credit and revenue can reduce requirements.

What documentation do I need?

Expect to provide business tax returns, profit and loss statements, bank statements, a driver's license, and equipment details (invoice, spec sheet, or listing). Some programs require fewer documents for smaller amounts.

How long does commercial vehicle financing approval take?

Simple applications can be approved in 1–3 business days. Complex deals, especially for larger amounts or weaker credit, may take 1–2 weeks. Pre-approvals can speed the process once equipment is selected.
