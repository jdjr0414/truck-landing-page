Financing for Food Trucks & Mobile Kitchens

Food truck financing covers the vehicle, the kitchen build-out, and the cooking equipment that turn a chassis into a working mobile restaurant. It funds new turnkey trucks, used units, and custom builds for caterers, concession operators, and first-time food entrepreneurs. Because a food truck is both a vehicle and a commercial kitchen, lenders can finance the whole package or split the truck and equipment into separate loans.

Definition

A food truck is a self-contained vehicle outfitted as a commercial kitchen for preparing and selling food on the move. A typical build includes cooking appliances (griddle, fryer, range, or oven), refrigeration, a three-compartment sink, a fresh and gray water system, ventilation with a fire-suppression hood, and an onboard generator or shore-power hookup. Food trucks serve street vending, catering, festivals, breweries, and private events. Most are built on a step-van, box-truck, or cutaway chassis and stay under 26,000 lbs GVWR.

How Food Truck Financing Works

A food truck loan is a form of commercial equipment financing secured by the truck itself. The lender advances the purchase price, you make fixed monthly payments over the term, and the vehicle serves as collateral. What makes food trucks different from a plain delivery vehicle is the build-out: the kitchen equipment often costs as much as the chassis. Lenders handle this in one of two ways. A turnkey loan finances the finished truck, vehicle and kitchen together, as a single amount, which is the simplest path when you buy a completed unit from a builder. A split structure finances the vehicle on one agreement and the kitchen equipment (appliances, refrigeration, generator, hood system) on a separate equipment loan or lease, which can help when you supply your own chassis and have it built out.

Because a mobile kitchen is a working income-producing asset, most lenders underwrite it like a business investment rather than a personal vehicle. They look at whether the truck's expected revenue can support the payment, alongside your credit and time in business. New buyers should get the build quote in writing early, since the total financed amount drives both the down payment and the monthly payment.

What Lenders Require

Food truck lenders evaluate the borrower and the equipment. Expect to provide most of the following:

  • Credit: Personal credit is the primary factor for startups. Established businesses add business credit. Most competitive programs want 640+; 700+ earns the best terms.
  • Time in business: Established food businesses (2+ years) qualify most easily. Startups can still qualify but face higher down payments and rates.
  • Down payment: Usually 10–30%, lower for strong credit and established revenue.
  • Equipment details: The truck's year, make, model, mileage, and a build quote or invoice listing the kitchen equipment.
  • Documentation: Driver's license, and for larger amounts, business bank statements, tax returns, and sometimes a simple business plan or menu concept.
  • Business plan (startups): Many first-time programs want a short plan covering the concept, target locations or events, and projected revenue.

Financing the Kitchen Build-Out

The build-out is what separates food truck financing from ordinary vehicle loans. The cooking line, refrigeration, ventilation hood with fire suppression, three-compartment sink, water and propane systems, electrical, and the exterior wrap can easily match or exceed the cost of the truck itself. There are two common ways to finance it.

With a turnkey purchase from a food truck builder, the entire finished unit is financed as one amount, so the kitchen is baked into the loan automatically. With a custom build, where you supply a chassis and hire a builder, lenders can finance the equipment as its own line, sometimes as an equipment loan and sometimes as an equipment lease. Leasing the kitchen equipment can lower the upfront cost and may offer tax advantages, while a loan builds ownership. Keep the builder's itemized quote, lenders use it to verify value and to structure the advance. If you plan to add equipment later (a second fryer, a larger generator), mention it up front so the financing can be sized correctly.

Startup and First-Time Owner Guidance

New food-truck businesses can get financed, they just face tighter terms than established operators. Lenders offset the added risk with larger down payments, shorter terms, and higher rates, and they lean heavily on personal credit. To improve your odds as a first-timer:

  • Lead with personal credit: With no business history, your personal score carries the approval. 680+ opens far more programs.
  • Bring a down payment: 15–30% down signals commitment and reduces the lender's exposure. It also lowers your monthly payment.
  • Write a simple business plan: Concept, service locations or event schedule, expected daily sales, and startup costs. It reassures underwriters.
  • Consider used or a concession trailer: A lower purchase price is easier to finance and less risky if the concept needs adjusting.
  • Document any food experience: Prior restaurant, catering, or vending experience strengthens a startup application.

New vs. Used Food Truck Financing

New turnkey food trucks qualify for the longest terms (up to 72 months), the lowest rates, and higher advance rates, and they come with warranties on both the chassis and the kitchen equipment. Used food truck financing is more restrictive: terms of 36–60 months, higher rates, and lower advances. Lenders weigh the truck's age, mileage, and the condition and age of the kitchen equipment, an older truck with a recently rebuilt kitchen may finance better than a newer truck with worn-out appliances. Get a used unit inspected, and confirm the hood suppression system and propane setup meet current code before you commit.

Section 179 and Tax Considerations

A food truck used for business is a depreciable asset, and the equipment build-out often qualifies for the Section 179 deduction, which lets a business expense the cost of qualifying equipment in the year it is placed in service rather than depreciating it over many years. Financed equipment can still qualify, you may be able to deduct the eligible cost while paying for the truck over time. Annual dollar limits, business-use requirements, and bonus depreciation rules apply and change year to year. This page is general information, not tax advice; confirm eligibility and current limits with a qualified tax professional and see IRS guidance on Section 179.

Licensing and Regulatory Requirements

Operating a food truck involves both vehicle rules and food-service rules. The following is general guidance, requirements vary by state and locality, and mobile-food businesses should verify with their state motor vehicle agency, local health department, and fire marshal.

Driver License Requirements

Most food trucks are built on chassis that stay under 26,000 pounds GVWR, so a standard driver's license is usually sufficient and no CDL is required. Very large mobile kitchens, or a heavy kitchen trailer towed behind a large truck, can cross the 26,000-lb threshold and require a Class B (or, for some combinations, Class A) CDL. Weight is the deciding factor, confirm the truck's GVWR before assuming no CDL is needed.

Food Service and Health Permits

Food trucks require permits that ordinary commercial vehicles do not. These commonly include a mobile food vendor permit, a health department permit and inspection, a food handler or manager certification, a commissary agreement (a licensed base kitchen many jurisdictions require), and a fire inspection of the suppression hood and propane system. Requirements differ sharply between cities and counties, some allow street vending broadly, others restrict it heavily.

DOT and Vehicle Registration

Food trucks operating locally usually register as commercial vehicles without needing a USDOT number. A USDOT number is generally tied to interstate transport of goods for hire and heavy vehicles, most single-location food trucks do not require one, but requirements vary by state and vehicle weight. Verify with your state motor vehicle agency.

Weight-Based Licensing Thresholds

Federal CDL requirements apply to vehicles with a GVWR of 26,001 pounds or more, or combination vehicles with a GCWR of 26,001 pounds or more. Most food trucks stay below this, but a fully loaded heavy build with water tanks, propane, and equipment can approach the limit. If you tow a concession trailer, the combined weight can also trigger CDL rules. Check both the truck's GVWR and any towed unit before operating.

Typical Experience or Training Expectations

Food truck operators typically need food-service credentials rather than driving certifications:

  • Food handler card: Required for staff handling food in most jurisdictions.
  • Food manager certification: Often required for the person in charge (e.g., ServSafe).
  • Fire safety: Hood suppression and propane systems must pass fire inspection; some cities require operator fire-safety training.
  • Allergen and sanitation training: Increasingly required by local health codes.
  • Commissary use: Many areas require food prep and overnight storage at a licensed commissary.

Can You Operate This Vehicle Without a CDL?

Usually yes. Most food trucks stay under 26,000 pounds GVWR and can be driven on a standard license. Larger mobile kitchens or heavy trailer combinations over the threshold require a Class B CDL. Confirm your vehicle's GVWR and any towed weight.

Disclaimer: Licensing, health, and fire rules for mobile food businesses vary by state, county, and city, and change over time. Businesses should verify current requirements with their state motor vehicle agency, local health department, fire marshal, and business licensing authority before operating. Common Questions

How much does a food truck cost?

New food trucks with a built-out kitchen typically run $75,000–$175,000. Used food trucks range from $30,000–$100,000. A bare vehicle plus a custom build often lands between $50,000 and $130,000, and a towed concession trailer can start near $20,000.

Can you finance the food truck build-out and kitchen equipment?

Yes. Many lenders finance the turnkey unit, including the vehicle, kitchen build-out, cooking equipment, refrigeration, generator, and wrap. Others structure the vehicle and equipment as separate loans or lease the kitchen equipment. Keep the builder's itemized quote to verify value.

Can a startup or first-time owner finance a food truck?

Yes. Startups typically need 10–30% down, strong personal credit (often 650–680+), and a short business plan. Some lenders offer new-business programs with higher rates. See startup business financing.

What credit score is needed to finance a food truck?

Many lenders prefer 640–680+ for competitive rates, and 700+ qualifies for the best terms. Specialty lenders may work with 580–640 with a larger down payment and higher rate.

What interest rates apply to food truck loans?

Rates generally run 7%–20% APR. Established businesses with strong credit see 7%–12%; fair credit runs 12%–16%; startups and challenged credit often see 16%–20% or more.

How long are food truck financing terms?

Terms commonly run 36–72 months. New turnkey trucks may reach 60–72 months; older used trucks are often capped at 36–48 months based on age and equipment condition.

How much down payment is required for a food truck?

Typically 10–20% for established businesses and 20–30% for startups or challenged credit. Strong credit and time in business can sometimes reduce this to little or no money down.

Should I buy new, used, or build custom?

New turnkey trucks finance most easily with the longest terms and a warranty. Used trucks cost less but face shorter terms and inspection scrutiny. Custom builds let you match the truck to your menu but require coordinating vehicle and equipment financing.

Can I get a food truck loan through the equipment builder?

Sometimes. Many food truck builders partner with lenders for turnkey financing. Comparing an independent commercial lender against the builder's offer helps you confirm you are getting competitive terms.

Do you need a CDL to drive a food truck?

Usually not. Most food trucks stay under 26,000 lbs GVWR and can be driven on a standard license. Very large mobile kitchens or heavy trailer combinations over the threshold require a Class B CDL.

What permits do I need to run a food truck?

Commonly a mobile food vendor permit, a health department permit and inspection, food handler or manager certification, a commissary agreement, and a fire inspection of the suppression hood and propane system. Requirements vary sharply by city and county.

How quickly can food truck financing be approved?

Pre-approval often takes 24–72 hours. Full approval and funding typically run 1–5 business days once documentation and the equipment quote are in. Have credit, bank statements, and the build quote ready.

Can Section 179 apply to a food truck?

Often yes. A food truck and its kitchen equipment used for business can qualify for the Section 179 deduction, even when financed. Annual limits and business-use rules apply and change yearly, confirm with a tax professional.
