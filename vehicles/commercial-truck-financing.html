Commercial Truck Financing: Rates, Terms & How to Get Approved

Commercial truck financing is a business loan or equipment lease used to purchase work trucks—from semi tractors and box trucks to dump, tow, and vocational units. The truck itself serves as collateral, which keeps rates lower than unsecured business credit. Whether you run one truck or a growing fleet, financing spreads the cost over 24 to 84 months so the equipment can earn while you pay it off.

Definition

Commercial truck financing is the use of a secured loan or equipment lease to acquire trucks for business use. The lender advances the purchase price, the truck acts as collateral, and the business repays over a fixed term with interest. It applies across every commercial truck category—over-the-road tractors, straight trucks, vocational and construction units, and specialty vehicles—so buyers can preserve working capital instead of paying cash.

How Commercial Truck Financing Works

The mechanics are the same regardless of truck type. You identify the truck and its price, apply with a lender or a matching service, and the lender underwrites the deal based on your credit, time in business, revenue, and the equipment itself. Because the truck secures the loan, approval is often faster and rates lower than for unsecured financing. Once approved, funds go to the dealer or seller, you take delivery, and you begin fixed monthly payments. Most commercial truck loans are simple-interest, fully amortizing structures with no prepayment penalty from many lenders—so paying ahead saves interest.

Two things drive the offer more than anything else: the strength of the borrower and the quality of the collateral. A profitable carrier buying a two-year-old tractor from a dealer will see very different terms than a brand-new authority buying a high-mileage truck from a private seller. Understanding both levers helps you shop realistically.

Down Payment Options

Down payments on commercial trucks range from $0 to about 20% for most borrowers, and they are risk-based rather than fixed. Strong credit and an established, profitable business frequently qualify with no money down. As credit weakens or the business is newer, lenders ask for more cash up front to reduce their exposure. A larger down payment is also a tool—putting more down often unlocks a lower rate or a longer term, even when zero-down is available.

  • $0 down: Common for 720+ credit and 2+ years in business with solid revenue.
  • 5–10% down: Typical for good credit or first-time buyers with strong personal credit.
  • 10–20% down: Fair credit, startups, or older equipment.
  • 20%+ down: Rebuilding credit or private-party purchases of older trucks.

Requirements and Documents Lenders Want

Commercial truck lenders keep documentation light for smaller loans and application-only approvals, then ask for more as the loan size grows. Having these ready speeds approval:

  • Business identification: EIN, entity formation documents, and business address.
  • Personal identification: Driver's license for each owner or guarantor.
  • Bank statements: Typically 3–6 months of business bank statements.
  • Tax returns: 1–2 years of business (and sometimes personal) returns for larger loans.
  • Equipment details: Make, model, year, VIN, mileage, and the seller's invoice or quote.
  • Proof of revenue or contracts: Especially for owner-operators and startups—load history, carrier agreements, or letters of intent.

Many lenders offer "application-only" approvals up to a set dollar amount (often $75,000–$150,000) that require little more than the application and a credit pull. Above that threshold, expect a full financial package.

New vs. Used Commercial Trucks

New trucks earn the best financing: longer terms, lower rates, higher advance rates, and access to manufacturer captive programs with promotional pricing. The trade-off is a higher purchase price and faster early depreciation. Used trucks lower the entry cost and let you buy more capability for the money, but lenders apply shorter terms and slightly higher rates, and they scrutinize mileage, age, and condition. A well-maintained, low-mileage used truck from a dealer can finance nearly as well as new; a high-mileage private-party truck will not. Match the purchase to how long you plan to keep the truck and how many miles you run annually.

Lease vs. Loan

Businesses finance commercial trucks two main ways: an equipment loan (you own the truck, it is collateral, you build equity) or an equipment lease (you use the truck for a term, often with a purchase option at the end). Loans suit operators who keep trucks long term and want to own the asset outright. Leases suit businesses that rotate equipment frequently, want lower payments, or prefer to preserve capital and credit lines. A $1 buyout lease functions much like a loan with ownership at the end, while a fair-market-value (FMV) lease keeps payments lowest and returns or buys the truck at market price when the term ends.

Section 179 and Tax Treatment

Commercial trucks used for business are generally eligible for the Section 179 deduction, which lets qualifying businesses deduct the cost of qualifying equipment placed in service during the tax year, subject to annual limits. Financed and leased equipment can qualify, which means a business may deduct the equipment cost while still spreading payments over time. Bonus depreciation may also apply. Tax rules change and depend on your situation—confirm eligibility and current limits with a qualified tax professional before relying on any deduction.

Financing Multiple Trucks and Fleets

Businesses adding capacity rarely stop at one truck. Fleet financing lets you fund several trucks under a single facility rather than applying loan by loan. Common structures include a master lease line (draw against a pre-approved amount as you buy), a business line of credit for equipment, or a portfolio loan covering multiple units. Underwriting shifts from a single asset to the health of the whole operation—total revenue, cash flow, existing debt, and combined equipment value. Established carriers can often secure a line that pre-approves them to buy trucks quickly when the right unit or contract appears. See trucking company financing for fleet-level guidance.

Financing for Startup Carriers

New authorities and first-year businesses can finance commercial trucks, but underwriting is stricter because there is little or no business history to lean on. Startups typically need strong personal credit, a down payment of 10–30%, and proof that revenue is coming—signed contracts, carrier agreements, or verifiable load history. Specialty lenders and first-time-buyer programs exist specifically for this segment and weigh the driver's experience and the truck's collateral value heavily. If your credit is strong and you can show contracted work, approval is realistic even in year one. See startup trucking business financing and the guide to starting a trucking company.

How to Get Approved Fast

Speed comes from preparation. Lenders can pre-approve in 24–72 hours and fund in 1–5 business days when nothing is missing. To move quickly:

  • Have documents ready: Bank statements, tax returns, and ID assembled before you apply.
  • Pin down the truck: A specific VIN, price, and seller invoice let the lender value the collateral immediately.
  • Know your numbers: Time in business, annual revenue, and existing equipment debt.
  • Match to the right lender: Credit tier and truck age determine which programs fit—applying to the wrong lender wastes time and can add hard inquiries.
  • Respond quickly: Underwriters often have one or two follow-up questions; same-day answers keep the file moving.

Common Questions

What interest rates can I expect for commercial truck financing?

Rates typically range from 6% to 20% APR. Strong-credit borrowers (720+) often see 6–9%; mid-tier credit (650–699) runs roughly 9–14%; challenged credit or startups may see 14–20% or need larger down payments. Manufacturer programs on new trucks can run lower.

How long are commercial truck financing terms?

Terms generally run 24 to 84 months. New trucks qualify for the longest terms (60–84 months); used trucks are usually 36–60 months, and older or high-mileage units 24–48 months.

How much down payment is required?

Down payments range from 0% to about 20% for most borrowers and are risk-based. Strong credit and established businesses often qualify with $0 down; startups and challenged credit typically need 10–20% or more.

Should I finance a new or used commercial truck?

New trucks get longer terms, lower rates, and higher advance rates, plus access to manufacturer programs. Used trucks cost less up front but carry shorter terms and slightly higher rates. Lenders weigh age, mileage, and resale value.

What documents do lenders want for commercial truck financing?

Commonly 1–2 years of business tax returns, 3–6 months of bank statements, a driver's license, and equipment details (make, model, year, VIN, price). Smaller application-only loans may need only the application and a credit pull.

How fast can commercial truck financing be funded?

Pre-approval often takes 24–72 hours, and full approval plus funding typically completes in 1–5 business days once documentation and the specific truck are confirmed.

Can I finance multiple trucks or a full fleet?

Yes. Fleet financing, equipment lines of credit, and master lease structures fund several trucks under one facility. Underwriting focuses on total revenue, cash flow, and combined equipment value rather than a single asset.

Can a startup carrier get commercial truck financing?

Yes. New authorities and startups can finance trucks, usually with 10–30% down, strong personal credit, and proof of contracts or load history. Specialty and first-time-buyer programs focus on this segment.

Is a lease or a loan better for a commercial truck?

A loan builds ownership and equity and suits long-term keepers. A lease keeps payments lower and suits businesses that rotate equipment frequently or want to preserve capital. A $1 buyout lease behaves like a loan; an FMV lease keeps payments lowest.

What credit score do I need to finance a commercial truck?

Many lenders prefer 600+ for approval and 680–720+ for the best rates. Specialty lenders work with lower scores when the borrower brings a larger down payment, strong collateral, or solid cash flow.

Can I write off a commercial truck on my taxes?

Business-use commercial trucks are generally eligible for the Section 179 deduction and possible bonus depreciation, including financed and leased equipment, subject to annual limits. Confirm eligibility and current limits with a tax professional.

Does the truck type change my financing options?

Yes. Cost, expected useful life, and resale market vary by truck type, which affects terms and advance rates. See the guide for your specific truck—semi, box, dump, and more—for details.
